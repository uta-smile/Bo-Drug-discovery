\begin{abstract}
Historical assays from related protein targets can accelerate the search
for active compounds, but an improvement in hit recovery does not show
that activity information has transferred between targets. We introduce
controls that separate activity transfer from model fitting, acquisition,
and historical compound coverage in multitask Bayesian optimization (BO):
response permutation at fixed assay locations, a crossed design of
Gaussian Process fitting against posterior conditioning, and historical
sampling controls. Across six BindingDB protein families, measured
auxiliary responses outperform permuted ones, with a Recall@10 difference
of \crossPrimary{} percentage points that favors measured responses in
all six families. Fitting on measured responses helps when the posterior
conditions on measured activities and hurts under permuted conditioning
(interaction \crossInteraction{} points, positive in all six families),
because measured fitting raises the learned coupling between targets
from \couplingPermuted{} to \couplingMeasured{}, in line with each
family's activity correlation (Spearman \couplingVsCorr{}). Separately,
a selection control that never reads auxiliary activities reproduces
\avoidRecallFracHundred{} of a CYP pooling reversal that occurs when the
historically assayed compounds change. Our results suggest caution when
reading multitask BO gains as evidence of biological transfer unless
response permutation and sampling controls are reported.
Code and reproduction instructions are available at
\url{https://github.com/uta-smile/Bo-Drug-discovery}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Finding compounds that act on a protein target is a central task in early stage small molecule discovery, yet experimental screening can evaluate only a fraction of a large chemical library~\cite{reker2015active}. Drug discovery campaigns also generate bioactivity measurements for related proteins, providing a potential source of information before a new target has been extensively assayed~\cite{paolini2006global}. Such measurements are biologically appealing because related targets may recognize overlapping chemical scaffolds, but their ligand preferences and activity profiles are not identical~\cite{keiser2007relating,davis2011comprehensive}. A compound active against one family member is therefore not guaranteed to be active against another. The central challenge is to exploit historical measurements without mistaking similarity between targets for interchangeability.

Bayesian optimization (BO) addresses limited assay budgets by iteratively selecting compounds using a predictive model and an acquisition function \cite{jones1998ego,shahriari2016taking,hie2020leveraging,bellamy2022batched}. In molecular BO, early selections are particularly consequential because relatively few measurements are available for the target of interest \cite{graff2021accelerating,gao2022pmo}. Multitask models can incorporate assays collected for related proteins and may improve the recovery of active compounds without consuming additional primary target assays \cite{zhao2020multitask,swersky2013multitask,ramsundar2015massively}. However, improved hit recovery is not, by itself, evidence that a model has learned useful compound activity relationships across targets~\cite{xu2017demystifying}. Auxiliary observations can also change Gaussian Process (GP) parameter estimation, posterior uncertainty, and acquisition decisions. Even which compounds appear in a historical assay panel can influence the search. Consequently, the same observed gain can support very different conclusions about the biological information being transferred.

Previous work has developed multitask GP models for sharing information across tasks \cite{bonilla2007multitask,swersky2013multitask,tighineanu2021transfer}, methods that learn GP priors from previous optimization tasks \cite{wang2024hyperbo}, and approaches for combining measurements with differing fidelity or cost \cite{mcdonald2025multifidelity}. Recent analyses distinguish shared parameter transfer from transfer through correlated function values \cite{sedgwick2025transfer}, and show that response standardization and limited observation overlap complicate task correlation inference \cite{hvarfner2026pitfalls}. These advances motivate a complementary question for molecular screening: when related target assays improve the sequence of selected compounds, how much of that improvement requires the measured activities to remain associated with the correct molecules? To our knowledge, no study of multitask BO has permuted auxiliary activities while holding their assay locations fixed, or crossed the responses used for GP fitting with those used for posterior conditioning.

We investigate this question through controlled retrospective screening experiments using BindingDB measurements \cite{bindingdb2016} from six protein families: JAK, CYP, DPP, HDAC, MMP, and PI3K. In each family, one protein is treated as the primary screening target and three as sources of historical auxiliary assays, with each protein taking the primary role in turn. We first permute auxiliary activity values within each target while keeping their empirical distributions and observed compound locations fixed. We then cross the responses used for GP parameter fitting with those used for posterior conditioning, allowing us to study their interaction in full sequential campaigns. Matched state replays additionally examine immediate compound selection with the same primary observations. Finally, we test uncertainty rescaled acquisition and changes to historical compound coverage, including a selection control that uses no auxiliary response values (Fig.~\ref{fig:design}).

The experiments are organized to move from detecting useful activity
correspondence to explaining how the surrogate and the sampled historical
compounds influence screening. Our central message is that improved hit
recovery is not, by itself, evidence of useful biological transfer, and
that specific matched comparisons reveal what historical assays actually
contribute. We contribute these comparisons as a reusable set of
statistical controls for any multitask BO campaign, not as a new
optimizer.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
The main contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Testing whether auxiliary activities match the right molecules.}
A response permutation control preserves historical compounds and
activity distributions while breaking their association. This tests
whether the observed screening advantage requires the measured activity
of each compound, rather than just the presence of historical assays.
Measured responses outperform permuted ones by a Recall@10 difference
of \crossPrimary{} points, in all six families.

\item \textbf{Separating GP fitting from posterior conditioning.}
A four policy experiment independently varies auxiliary responses used
to fit GP parameters and to condition the posterior. The resulting
interaction and matched decision replays identify when measured
responses change the compounds selected for screening. The interaction
is \crossInteraction{} points and positive in all six families, and the
fitted coupling links it to the measured activity similarity of each
protein family.

\item \textbf{Explaining gains that survive response randomization.}
Acquisition and historical sampling controls assess how screening can
change without correct auxiliary activity assignments. A basis transfer
control then tests whether this sensitivity differs when auxiliary
predictions enter the primary GP as features instead of direct
conditioning observations. A selection control that never reads
auxiliary activities reproduces \avoidRecallFracHundred{} of the CYP
pooling reversal.

\end{enumerate}

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:framework}

\subsection{Screening Setting and BO Models}
\label{sec:matrix}

We consider a fixed compound library $\mathcal M$ with one
primary protein target $t_0$ and three related auxiliary
targets $t_1,t_2,t_3$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:design}).
Historical auxiliary assays are available before screening,
whereas primary target activities are revealed only when
compounds are selected. The assay budget applies exclusively
to the primary target, and missing outcomes are not imputed.

Compounds are represented by 2048 bit ECFP4 fingerprints
\cite{rogers2010ecfp,rdkit} using a Tanimoto kernel~\cite{ralaivola2005graph}.
We compare four Gaussian Process (GP)~\cite{rasmussen2006gp} strategies:
single task BO (\stl{}), trained only on primary target
observations; multitask BO (\mt{}), which estimates
cross target coupling; fully pooled BO (\pooled{}),
which assumes complete coupling between targets; and
basis transfer BO (\basis{}), which uses predictions from separate
auxiliary GPs as features for the primary model and is described in
Section~\ref{sec:method-controls}.

For the multitask GP~\cite{bonilla2007multitask,alvarez2012kernels}, covariance is defined as
\[
k\big((m,a),(m',b)\big)
= k_{\mathrm{Tanimoto}}(m,m')B_{ab},
\qquad
B=(1-r)I+r\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top},
\]
where $r\in[0,1]$ controls the shared coupling between
targets and is selected by marginal likelihood~\cite{rasmussen2006gp}
(Supplement~S1.1). Target identity enters through $B$;
protein sequence and structural features are not used.

GP parameters are fitted using separately standardized
target responses. Primary target statistics are computed
only from observed campaign data and updated after each
refit, preventing information leakage from unassayed
compounds. Full pooling therefore assumes a shared
activity function on the standardized scale.
Auxiliary observations may also influence shared
parameter fitting when $r=0$ (Supplement~S1).

Compounds are selected using Expected Improvement (EI)~\cite{jones1998ego}
relative to the best observed primary target activity,
with offset $\xi=0.01$ in pIC$_{50}$ units. GP predictions
are returned to the primary target activity scale before
acquisition. The posterior remains fixed within each
batch and is updated after the selected compounds are
evaluated. Additional controls modify the uncertainty
input to EI or penalize compounds covered by historical
assays. The molecular representations, surrogate strategies,
and sequential acquisition process are summarized
in Fig.~\ref{fig:bo-pipeline}.

\subsection{BindingDB Panels and Evaluation}
\label{sec:setup}

We evaluate six protein families from BindingDB
\cite{bindingdb2016,liu2025bindingdb}: JAK, CYP, DPP, HDAC, MMP, and
PI3K. These families provide biologically relevant
settings for auxiliary data transfer, as related
proteins may share compound activity patterns while
retaining distinct ligand preferences.

Each family contains four protein targets. We retain
compounds with activity measurements available across
all four targets, allowing each target to serve as the
primary screening task while the remaining three
provide auxiliary histories. This complete panel
design enables controlled retrospective comparisons
without imputing unmeasured activities.

Recall@10 is the fraction of the ten compounds with highest
measured primary target activity that a campaign discovers within its
primary assay budget. Differences in Recall@10 are reported in
percentage points. Each family is evaluated at two primary assay
budgets (12 family and budget settings), starting from 10 random
compounds and selecting batches of 10; each setting averages 128
campaigns, 32 per primary target (Supplement~S1.5).

Family level inference uses a two sided Wilcoxon signed rank test over
the six family effects, reported with 95\% $t$ intervals; targets,
campaigns, and budgets are aggregated within families rather than
counted as independent replicates. With six families, the smallest
attainable $p$ value is $0.031$, reached only when all six effects share
a sign. The C1 permutation contrast and the C2 interaction are the
primary tests; the remaining tests are exploratory and are not adjusted
for multiplicity.

The resulting panels contain between 248 and 904
compounds. Although this design supports matched
comparisons, it favors densely characterized
compound target relationships and may not reflect
the sparsity of prospective screening data.
Target identities, library sizes, and within family
activity correlations are provided in Supplement~S1.3.



\subsection{Controls for Interpreting Auxiliary Data Gains}
\label{sec:method-controls}

We use five complementary controls to distinguish the
effects of auxiliary activity correspondence, model
fitting, and compound selection. These controls provide
diagnostic comparisons rather than a unique causal
decomposition of optimization gains.

\paragraph{C1. Response Correspondence Control.}
We permute activity values within each auxiliary target,
preserving historical observation locations, response
distributions, and all primary target measurements.
Locations are sampled before permutation, with one
fixed permutation per auxiliary target and replicate.
This tests whether hit recovery depends on the correct
compound activity pairing~\cite{rucker2007yrandomization,lindgren1996permutation}. A complementary
Gaussian noise control varies response information
from measured ($q=1$) to randomized ($q=0$)
(Supplement~S1.2).

\paragraph{C2. Crossed Fitting and Conditioning Experiment.}
We independently vary the auxiliary responses used
for GP parameter fitting and posterior conditioning,
producing four policies: $MM$, $MP$, $PM$, and $PP$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:design}). Here $M$ denotes measured
responses and $P$ denotes within target permutations;
the first letter specifies fitting and the second
conditioning.

Our primary diagnostic is the fitting and conditioning
interaction:
\[
\Delta_{\mathrm{FC}}
= (R_{MM}-R_{PM})-(R_{MP}-R_{PP}),
\]
where $R_{AB}$ denotes Recall@10 under policy $AB$.

All four policies start with the same primary observations
and historical compound locations. Each then selects its
own compounds and refits its model during the campaign.
For policies that use a fitted model from another condition,
posterior conditioning retains those fitted parameters.

We additionally perform next batch replays at matched
\stl{} and $MM$ states. Under a fixed fit, changing
conditioning responses alters posterior means but
not predictive variances, allowing us to examine
response dependent compound selection independently
of accumulated campaign differences.

\paragraph{C3. Uncertainty Rescaling Control.}
To test whether altered exploration can reproduce
a multitask gain, we retain the single task model
and rescale only the predictive standard deviation
used by Expected Improvement:
\[
\widetilde{\sigma}(m)
= c\,\sigma_{\stl}(m).
\]
The scaling factor $c$ is based on early
uncertainty ratios between \mt{} and \stl{};
it is not tuned to maximize recall. This changes acquisition behavior
without introducing auxiliary observations into the
single task posterior.

\paragraph{C4. Historical Sampling Control.}
We compare uniformly sampled auxiliary histories with
\emph{withheld histories} that exclude the primary
target's true top ten compounds. Uniform sampling is
our primary evaluation; withholding uses unavailable
outcome information and serves only as a retrospective
stress test. We additionally sample \emph{preferential
histories}, in which a compound enters the history with
probability proportional to $\exp(\alpha z)$ for $z$ its
standardized mean auxiliary activity. Here $\alpha=0$
recovers uniform sampling, while $\alpha=1$ favors
compounds with higher auxiliary activity. Unlike
withholding, this design uses no primary target outcomes
(Supplement~S2).

To examine history dependent selection without directly
using auxiliary activity values, we multiply single task
EI by $W\in(0,1)$ for compounds included in the auxiliary
history, leaving other compounds unchanged. We calibrate
$W$ to match the full pooling policy's normalized
historical compound selection frequency and compare
recall across sampling designs (Supplement~S2).

\paragraph{C5. Basis Transfer Control.}
The final control asks whether history sampling matters as much
when auxiliary predictions are used as model features rather than
as additional observations. Rather than include
auxiliary assay rows directly in the primary GP conditioning set,
we fit a separate GP for each auxiliary target. Their standardized
posterior mean predictions define three features
$h(m)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}$ for compound $m$. The primary model is
\[
f(m)=h(m)^{\top}\beta+g(m),\qquad
g\sim\mathcal{GP}(0,k_{\mathrm{Tanimoto}}),\qquad
\beta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\tau^{2}I).
\]
Marginalizing $\beta$ gives the kernel
\[
k_{\mathrm{basis}}(m,m')=
k_{\mathrm{Tanimoto}}(m,m')
+\tau^{2}h(m)^{\top}h(m'),
\]
with hyperparameters fitted by marginal likelihood.
Auxiliary activity information thus reaches the primary GP through
predictive features, not through direct conditioning on auxiliary assay
rows. The comparison
therefore tests whether the route used to incorporate auxiliary data
changes sensitivity to the sampling design~\cite{tighineanu2021transfer}.



\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\paragraph{Multitask BO helps in every family; full pooling does not.}
Under uniform historical sampling, \mt{} improves Recall@10 over
\stl{} in all 12 family and budget settings, with a mean gain of
\valMtUniform{} percentage points across the six families. Complete
pooling is less consistent: it improves recovery in seven of the
12 settings and four of the six families. Its mean advantage is
\valPooledUniform{} points (95\% CI: \ciPooledUniform{}), without
a statistically significant overall gain. Thus, historical assays can
help, but assuming that related proteins share the same activity
function is not consistently beneficial. The following experiments
ask which features of the historical data produce these differences.

\subsection{Correct Assignment of Auxiliary Activities Improves Hit Recovery}
\label{sec:res-null}

An auxiliary assay should provide more than a list of compounds already
tested: its measurements must indicate which molecules are likely to
act on the primary target. C1 tests this by retaining every historical
compound and the observed activity distribution while permuting which
compound receives each auxiliary activity. With measured responses,
\mt{} improves Recall@10 over \stl{} by \crossMeasuredGain{}
percentage points. When the auxiliary responses are permuted for both
GP fitting and posterior conditioning, the gain changes to
\crossPermutedGain{} points. The difference between these policies
is \crossPrimary{} points (95\% CI: \crossPrimaryCi{};
$p=\crossPrimaryP{}$), and favors measured responses in
\crossPrimaryPositive{} of the six families.

Because the assay locations and activity distributions do not change,
the reduced gain cannot be explained solely by having more training
rows or by the overall range of historical activities. It shows that
the compound receiving each activity value matters for sequential
screening. However, permutation changes both the fitted GP parameters
and the observations used for posterior conditioning. C1 therefore
establishes that the response assignments matter, but not which use
of those responses accounts for the improvement.

\subsection{Useful GP Fitting Depends on Subsequent Conditioning}
\label{sec:res-mech}

The reduction after permutation motivates a more specific question:
does fitting GP parameters on measured auxiliary activities help
independently of the activities used to condition the posterior?
C2 crosses measured ($M$) and permuted ($P$) responses for these
two operations, with fitting indicated by the first letter
(Fig.~\ref{fig:interaction}).

When the posterior conditions on measured activities, fitting on
measured rather than permuted responses improves recall by
\crossFitMeasuredResponse{} percentage points ($MM-PM$; 95\% CI:
\crossFitMeasuredResponseCi{}). When conditioning instead uses
permuted responses, fitting on measured values changes recall by
\crossFitPermutedResponse{} points ($MP-PP$; 95\% CI:
\crossFitPermutedResponseCi{}). The fitting advantage thus reverses
with the conditioning data. The interaction between fitting and
conditioning is \crossInteraction{} points (95\% CI:
\crossInteractionCi{}) and is positive in
\crossInteractionPositive{} of the six families.

The same dependence appears when conditioning is the contrast of
interest. Replacing permuted conditioning with measured conditioning
changes recall by \crossResponseMeasuredFit{} points after measured
fitting ($MM-MP$; 95\% CI: \crossResponseMeasuredFitCi{}), compared
with \crossResponsePermutedFit{} points after permuted fitting
($PM-PP$; 95\% CI: \crossResponsePermutedFitCi{}).
The central result is not simply that real auxiliary responses are
better than randomized ones: a GP fitted on measured activities can
perform worse when its posterior is then conditioned on permuted
activities. Parameter fitting and conditioning must therefore be
considered together when interpreting apparent activity transfer.

\paragraph{The fitted coupling explains the interaction.}
Fitting on
measured responses gives a mean coupling of $\hat r=\,$\couplingMeasured{}
(family range \couplingMeasuredMin{} to \couplingMeasuredMax{}), whereas
fitting on permuted responses gives \couplingPermuted{}, with $\hat r=0$
in \couplingPermutedZeroPct{} of fitted states. A measured fit therefore
opens a route through which the conditioning responses reach the primary
posterior: measured conditioning then helps, and permuted conditioning
then hurts, more than under a permuted fit that largely ignores the
auxiliary rows. The interaction is thus a consequence of the learned
coupling rather than a separate effect. Across families, the measured
coupling tracks the median pairwise activity correlation between
targets (Spearman \couplingVsCorr{}; Supplement~S1.3), from
\couplingMeasuredLowFamily{} (correlation \couplingLowFamilyCorr{})
to \couplingMeasuredHighFamily{} (\couplingHighFamilyCorr{}), and the
conditioning benefit after measured fitting ($MM-MP$) follows the same
family order as $\hat r$ (Spearman \condVsCoupling{}). These six family
associations are descriptive, but they connect the interaction to a
biological quantity: the GP learns a coupling consistent with how
similarly the related proteins rank the same compounds, and that
coupling limits how much the measured activities can contribute.

\paragraph{Why the selected compounds change.}
The crossed policies also accumulate different primary measurements
as campaigns progress. To examine a single decision without those
differences, we replay the next batch using the same primary data and
fixed fitted parameters. At states taken from \stl{} campaigns, $MM$
and $MP$ propose batches containing on average
\crossStlAnchorMMBatchHits{} and \crossStlAnchorMPBatchHits{} of the
true top ten compounds. At states taken from $MM$ campaigns, the
corresponding averages are \crossMtAnchorMMBatchHits{} and
\crossMtAnchorMPBatchHits{}.

Within each replay, changing only the conditioning responses leaves
predictive variances unchanged but alters posterior means and the
proposed compounds. This supplies a direct example of how the measured
activity assignments affect an immediate screening decision. It does
not attribute the final campaign difference to posterior means alone,
because later assay choices and model refits also differ
(Supplement~S2).

\subsection{Acquisition and Historical Coverage Can Produce Gains Without Informative Response Pairings}
\label{sec:res-exploration}

\paragraph{Uncertainty changes explain some gains, but not all (C3).}
C1 and C2 establish a contribution from measured activity assignments.
They do not imply that every gain from auxiliary assays has that
origin. One alternative is that the GP changes the balance between
exploring uncertain compounds and favoring compounds with high
predicted activity. C3 tests this possibility by changing only the
uncertainty supplied to the \stl{} acquisition function. Gains that
survive randomization are clearest when the primary target's best
compounds are absent from history, so C3 uses the withheld history
stress test of C4; under uniform histories, the CYP \pooled{} gain with
randomized responses is instead \pooledRecallUniformFifty{} points.

On JAK, \mt{} has about $0.80$ times the predictive standard deviation
of \stl{} near 50 observed primary compounds. Scaling the \stl{}
uncertainty by $c=0.80$ improves Recall@10 by
\valScaleMatchedJak{}~$\pm$~\semScaleMatchedJak{} percentage points,
close to the \valMechJakMt{} point gain from \mt{} with randomized
auxiliary responses. Yet \mt{} with measured responses exceeds the
scaled \stl{} control by $5.31\pm0.91$ points. Thus, changing
acquisition behavior reproduces part of the JAK gain but does not
replace useful auxiliary activity information.

The same explanation fails on CYP. Across tested scaling factors
$c\in[0.35,20]$, the best \stl{} gain is only $+0.08$ points,
compared with \valMechCypPooled{} points for \pooled{} with
randomized responses. Changes in the acquisition uncertainty input
therefore account for some, but not all, gains that survive
randomization.

\paragraph{Which compounds were historically assayed matters (C4).}
If changing acquisition uncertainty does not explain the CYP gain,
another possibility is that historical observations change which
parts of the library the optimizer visits. We compare histories that
exclude the primary target's ten best compounds with uniformly
sampled histories. With 100 observations per auxiliary target
(300 in total) and randomized auxiliary responses, \pooled{} changes
from a $+22.97$ point advantage over \stl{} under withheld histories
to a $-23.05$ point disadvantage under uniform histories. This is
a 46.02 percentage point reversal (Fig.~\ref{fig:cyp-sampling}a).
At this size, the auxiliary histories cover \avoidCoverageHundred{} of
the 335 compound CYP library, so a uniform history almost always
contains the primary target's best compounds, whereas a withheld history
never does. The CYP reversal is therefore an extreme case of history
composition, and the six family estimate below gives its typical
magnitude.
Because the auxiliary responses are randomized, the reversal cannot
be attributed to correct measured response assignments in this
experiment. Withholding uses knowledge of the primary target's true
best compounds, so it is a retrospective stress test, not a feasible
prospective sampling strategy.

The strength of the reversal also changes with the amount of
historical coverage. Under withheld histories, increasing the total
auxiliary observations from 30 to 300 changes the randomized response
\pooled{} gain from $-1.25$ to $+22.97$ points. Across families,
switching from withheld to uniform histories changes the mean
\pooled{} advantage by \valPooledDesignChange{}~$\pm$~
\semPooledDesignChange{} points (95\% CI:
\ciPooledDesignChange{}), with a negative change in all six
families ($p=\pPooledDesignChange{}$). The analogous \mt{} change is
\valMtDesignChange{}~$\pm$~\semMtDesignChange{} points, with both
positive and negative effects across families (Supplement~S2.1).
These results show that the apparent value of fully pooled history
can depend on which compounds were assayed, not just on how many
assay results are available. The CYP experiment uses randomized
($q=0$) responses rather than the permutations used in C2, so the
magnitudes across those controls should not be compared directly.

We next examine a selection pattern consistent with this reversal.
Under uniform CYP histories, the frequency of selecting historical
compounds relative to their library coverage is $0.972$ for \stl{},
$0.542$ for \mt{}, and $0.288$ for \pooled{}
(Fig.~\ref{fig:cyp-sampling}b). For \pooled{}, the mean predictive
standard deviation is $0.412$ on historically assayed compounds,
compared with $0.542$ on other compounds. Lower uncertainty at
historical locations can make them less attractive to Expected
Improvement. Avoiding such compounds could help when the best primary
hits are absent from history, but could hurt when those hits are
included. These measurements suggest, but do not prove, this
explanation.

\paragraph{Selection behavior alone can reproduce much of the reversal.}
To test the proposed selection explanation more directly, C4 adds a
penalty to \stl{} Expected Improvement for compounds found in the
historical assays. Auxiliary activities are not used to score
compounds; the penalty strength is calibrated to reproduce the
historical compound selection frequency of \pooled{}.
On independent CYP campaigns, the target and achieved normalized
selection frequencies are \avoidTargetRatioHundred{} and
\avoidAchievedRatioHundred{} (within $0.003$), using
$W=\avoidWHundred{}$. This simple control reproduces
\avoidRecallFracHundred{} of the \pooled{} recall reversal:
\avoidRecallSwingHundred{} versus \pooledRecallSwingHundred{}
percentage points across withheld and uniform histories
(\avoidCampaignsHundred{} campaigns per condition). At coverage
\avoidCoverageFifty{}, it reproduces \avoidRecallFracFifty{} of the
sampling change.

The result matters because historical compound avoidance, even
without reading auxiliary activity values during acquisition, can
produce a substantial change in recovered hits. The control is
calibrated using \pooled{} behavior, so it demonstrates a possible
selection mechanism rather than measuring its unique contribution
to \pooled{} performance (Supplement~S2).

\paragraph{The transfer model changes sensitivity to sampling (C5).}
The avoidance control alters compound selection explicitly. C5 asks
whether sampling also has different effects when auxiliary predictions
enter the GP in a different way. For each model, we compare its
Recall@10 gain over \stl{} under preferential histories with its gain
under uniform histories. Denote this difference by $D(q)$, where
$q=1$ uses measured auxiliary responses and $q=0$ uses randomized
responses. A negative value means the model loses more of its
advantage over \stl{} under preferential sampling.

Preferential historical locations were originally chosen using
measured auxiliary activities. They may therefore remain
informative even after the activities attached to those locations
are randomized. The difference $D(1)-D(0)$ compares two experimental
conditions; it is not a separate causal estimate of information
transfer. The \decompValidityArms{} controls that do not read
auxiliary response values give identical results for $q=1$ and
$q=0$ when historical locations and campaigns are held fixed.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What counts as evidence of activity transfer.}
Related protein assays are useful only if their influence on screening
can be interpreted correctly. C1 shows that keeping activities matched
to the molecules that produced them matters for hit recovery. C2
adds an important qualification: using measured auxiliary data to fit
a GP is not automatically beneficial unless its subsequent
conditioning data support those fitted relationships. The positive
fitting and conditioning interaction across six families provides a
specific diagnostic for this dependence, and the fitted coupling
explains it: measured fitting raises $\hat r$ roughly in proportion to
how similarly the family's proteins rank compounds, which amplifies
whichever responses are then conditioned on. Together, these experiments
support a contribution from measured compound activities, rather than
attributing every multitask gain to shared biological function.

\paragraph{Why the historical assay panel matters.}
The CYP reversal illustrates a different failure mode. Even when
auxiliary response assignments are randomized, a joint GP can
favor compounds outside its historical training locations. Whether
that behavior improves recall depends on which molecules were
historically assayed. The response free avoidance control reproduces
much of this sensitivity, while the uncertainty scaling experiment
accounts for a JAK gain but not the CYP pooling result. No single
acquisition explanation fits every family, and the avoidance penalty
was calibrated from \pooled{} selections.

\paragraph{What the basis comparison adds.}
Under randomized responses, preferential sampling detectably reduces
the advantage of \mt{} and \pooled{}, but not of \basis{}, which does
not condition on individual auxiliary assay rows. This raises the
possibility that sampling sensitivity depends on the transfer
architecture, without establishing a significant difference between
architectures.

\paragraph{Biological meaning depends on the screening objective.}
Proteins in the same family often recognize related chemical features,
but may have different ligand preferences. Pooling their activities
therefore need not improve screening for a particular primary target
\cite{wang2019negative,xu2017demystifying}. Activity on another target
may be desirable for a multitarget drug, yet undesirable when
selectivity is important~\cite{paolini2006global,lankas2005dpp,vandenbroucke2014mmp}.
DPP illustrates both points: selectivity of DPP4 inhibitors over DPP8
and DPP9 is a recognized safety consideration~\cite{lankas2005dpp}, and
DPP has the weakest activity correlation and the smallest learned
coupling of the six families.
Historical assays for such targets may still be useful as constraints
rather than as positive training examples. The present experiments
optimize primary target Recall@10, not selectivity, so they do not
establish which auxiliary proteins should be used in a selective
drug discovery campaign.

\paragraph{Limits of the evidence.}
The study uses retrospective BindingDB panels of 248 to 904 compounds,
requiring measurements on all four targets. These panels allow matched
comparisons but favor densely characterized compounds and do not
represent the missing measurements common in prospective campaigns.
Withheld histories require knowledge
of the true primary hits and are only a stress test. The GP uses
ECFP4 fingerprints with a single nonnegative coupling parameter, so
neither the three dimensional structure of the molecules nor protein
sequence or structure enters the model. A structure aware surrogate
built on a state of the art molecular encoder may extract more from
historical assays than fingerprints do; whether the controls reach the
same verdicts under such a model is untested. We have not
tested prospective assay savings, generalization to broader chemical
spaces, or improvements in target selectivity.

\paragraph{Implications and future evaluation.}
A claim that auxiliary assays transfer useful activity information
should report, beyond final hit recovery:
(i) a response permutation control at fixed historical locations (C1);
(ii) the crossed fitting and conditioning contrast, together with the
fitted coupling (C2);
(iii) which compounds the auxiliary histories contain, with withheld
or other outcome dependent designs labelled as stress tests (C4);
(iv) a selection control that uses no auxiliary responses (C4); and
(v) whether changing the acquisition rule or transfer model changes the
result (C3, C5).
Future work could test these diagnostics on naturally sparse assay
histories and prospectively collected measurements. Sequence,
structure, and binding site representations may also help assess
which protein targets are relevant before transferring assays
\cite{vanwesten2011pcm,cortesciriano2015pcm}.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Historical assays improved molecular BO in several settings, but
those gains had different explanations. Correctly matched auxiliary
activities contributed to hit recovery, and the benefit of fitting
on measured responses depended on the values subsequently used for
posterior conditioning, through a learned coupling that tracked the
activity similarity of each protein family. At the same time, historical compound
coverage and acquisition behavior changed screening performance
even after auxiliary responses were randomized. The basis transfer
comparison showed that sampling sensitivity may also vary with how
auxiliary predictions enter the GP. Our results therefore suggest
caution when reading hit recovery gains from related target assays as
evidence of biological transfer unless a response permutation control
and the historical sampling design are reported.
