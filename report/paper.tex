\begin{abstract}
Historical activity measurements from related protein targets can guide
molecular Bayesian optimization (BO) when primary-target assays are scarce.
Yet improved compound discovery does not by itself establish informative
cross-target transfer: auxiliary data also affect surrogate fitting,
acquisition behavior, and the influence of historically measured compounds.

We examine these effects through retrospective screening experiments using
BindingDB data from six protein families. We compare single-task, fitted
multi-task, and fully pooled Gaussian-process BO under matched assay budgets
and historical observation locations. Four diagnostic controls test auxiliary
response correspondence, joint fitting, acquisition uncertainty, and
historical compound selection. Within-target permutation preserves the
response distributions actually observed in auxiliary history. Crossed
controls exchange conditioning responses while retaining the parameters
and normalization transforms of measured- or permuted-response fits.

Under uniform histories, fitted multi-task BO improves discovery across
all six families, and its advantage is reduced by historical-response
permutation. Measured-response fitting improves discovery when conditioning
also uses measured responses, but reduces it when conditioning responses
are permuted. Matched-history replays further show that response-dependent
predictive means change compound selection at identical predictive variance.
Other gains persist under randomized responses and can be reproduced by
covariance-free joint fitting, uncertainty-adjusted acquisition, or historical
compound avoidance. Full pooling is particularly sensitive to outcome-informed
historical sampling. These results distinguish routes of auxiliary response
information from alternative explanations for discovery gains and provide
a practical evaluation protocol without assigning unique causal mechanisms
or causal percentages to the observed effects.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Identifying active compounds for a protein target requires screening
molecular libraries under limited experimental budgets. Bayesian
optimization (BO) fits a surrogate to observed activities and uses an
acquisition function to select compounds for evaluation~\cite{jones1998ego,shahriari2016taking}.
With few primary-target measurements, both predictions and uncertainty
estimates depend strongly on the surrogate's modeling assumptions;
uncertainty calibration is also difficult to assess at small sample
sizes~\cite{foldager2023uncertainty}.

Historical activity measurements from related protein targets offer a way
to guide screening without purchasing additional primary-target assays.
Multi-task molecular prediction and BO use such measurements by sharing
information across targets~\cite{zhao2020multitask,swersky2013multitask}.
A natural evaluation compares discovery against single-task BO at the same
assay budget. However, a gain in discovery recall does not establish that
the measured auxiliary responses contain useful information about the
primary target.

Auxiliary data can affect BO through several routes. Joint fitting can
change shared hyperparameters and predictive uncertainty, thereby altering
acquisition decisions. Historical observation locations can also influence
which compounds the optimizer explores, independently of their auxiliary
responses. Distinguishing these effects is necessary to interpret a gain
as evidence of informative cross-target transfer.

We study this distinction through retrospective molecular BO experiments
on six BindingDB protein families. Figure~\ref{fig:matrix-methods} shows the
primary-target screening problem and the auxiliary history available to
multi-task models. Our comparisons pair discovery performance with controls
on response information, model fitting, acquisition uncertainty, and
historical sampling. The crossed experiment asks whether auxiliary activity information improves
discovery through posterior conditioning, through the parameters learned
during fitting, or through their interaction.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item We develop a reproducible diagnostic experiment that crosses auxiliary
response correspondence with fitted parameters and normalization transforms,
within a four-control framework for interpreting molecular BO gains.
\item Across six retrospective protein-family libraries under uniform
histories, measured-response fitting helps discovery with measured
conditioning responses but hurts with permuted responses; shared-state
replays show altered compound selection at identical predictive variance.
\item We show that outcome-informed exclusion of the primary target's best
compounds from history can reverse full pooling's apparent benefit, and
that an avoidance policy using no auxiliary responses reproduces much of
this sensitivity in separate calibrated runs.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Bayesian optimization for molecular discovery.}
Graff et al.~\cite{graff2021accelerating} studied active learning for
structure-based virtual screening, and Gao et al.~\cite{gao2022pmo}
emphasized evaluation under limited oracle budgets. McDonald
et al.~\cite{mcdonald2025multifidelity} combined computational and
experimental measurements through multifidelity BO. Our setting reuses
historical responses from related protein targets, rather than allocating
new evaluations across experimental fidelities.

\paragraph{Multi-task learning and knowledge transfer.}
Multi-task Gaussian processes combine a shared input kernel with a task
covariance matrix~\cite{bonilla2007multitask}. Swersky
et al.~\cite{swersky2013multitask} used this approach to reuse observations
across optimization tasks, while Sedgwick et al.~\cite{sedgwick2025transfer}
compared transfer-learning approaches for biological sequence design.
HyperBO learns GP priors from earlier tasks~\cite{wang2024hyperbo},
illustrating that auxiliary responses can inform fitting as well as
posterior conditioning. Hvarfner et al.~\cite{hvarfner2026pitfalls} showed that task relationships
can be difficult to estimate with limited data and observation overlap.
Our contribution is a diagnostic comparison of these routes in sequential
molecular screening, rather than a new permutation test or GP prior-learning
algorithm.

\paragraph{Validation and historical-data selection.}
Response randomization tests whether molecular predictive relationships
survive disruption of activity labels~\cite{rucker2007yrandomization}.
Training-data composition also affects model-guided biological
optimization~\cite{wittmann2021informed}, and relationships between protein
targets can introduce leakage into affinity benchmarks~\cite{leakage2026}.
Our controls extend these concerns to sequential screening, where both
auxiliary responses and historical observation locations influence
acquisition decisions.

\section{Diagnosing Auxiliary-Data Gains}
\label{sec:framework}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Experimental Setting and Assay Budget}
\label{sec:matrix}

We consider Bayesian optimization over a fixed compound
library $\mathcal M$, with one primary protein target
$t_0$ and related targets $t_1,\ldots,t_{T-1}$ providing
historical activity measurements. Let $y(m,t_j)$ denote
the activity of compound $m$ against target $t_j$,
with larger values indicating greater activity.
Available measurements are represented by a
compound--target observation mask
$M\in\{0,1\}^{N\times T}$; missing entries are
not imputed.

In each retrospective campaign, primary-target
activities are revealed only when their compounds
are selected for evaluation. Discovery performance
is measured against the ten most active compounds
in the constructed library, rather than against
an external or exhaustive chemical space.

We distinguish two uses of auxiliary measurements.
In the main transfer experiments, historical assays
have already been collected and do not consume the
new screening budget. Each newly evaluated
compound--target pair counts as one assay, and
the entire new budget is allocated to the primary
target. A separate study, reported in the
supplement, examines how a fixed budget for new
measurements should be divided across targets.

\subsection{Sources of Auxiliary-Data Effects}
An auxiliary history comprises observed compound--target
locations $\mathcal L$, their responses $y_{\mathrm{aux}}$,
and a sampling rule $\pi$, with
$\mathcal L=\{(m,t_j):M_{mj}=1,\ j\geq1\}$.
Responses may convey primary-target activity information;
observation locations can affect uncertainty and acquisition;
and joint fitting may change shared hyperparameters.
Consequently, a gain over single-task BO does not identify
its source. Controls C1--C4 test the original auxiliary
activity--compound correspondence and alternative explanations
in model fitting, acquisition, and historical selection.
Their effects need not be independent or additive.

\subsection{Models and Acquisition}
\label{sec:models}

Each observed compound--target entry supplies a training example
$((\mathbf{x}_m,t_j),y(m,t_j))$. The same compound may appear at several
targets with different responses. We use fixed 2048-bit ECFP4
fingerprints~\cite{rogers2010ecfp,rdkit} and a Tanimoto kernel, without
learned molecular features or protein sequence and structure features.
Cross-target dependence enters only through the task covariance matrix.

We compare three GP surrogates. Single-task
BO (\stl{}) uses only primary-target
measurements. Fitted multi-task BO (\mt{})
uses both primary and auxiliary measurements,
with cross-target dependence learned from the
data. Fully pooled BO (\pooled{}) uses the
same measurements but assumes complete
dependence across targets.

For the multi-task model, the covariance
between compound--target pairs is
\[
k\big((m,a),(m',b)\big)
=
k_{\mathrm{Tanimoto}}(m,m')B_{ab},
\qquad
B=(1-r)I+r\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}.
\]
The parameter $r$ controls the common
dependence between different targets.
At $r=0$, their direct covariance vanishes;
at $r=1$, they are modeled as fully coupled.
Thus, \mt{} estimates one shared coupling
parameter rather than a separate relationship
for each pair of proteins.

We select $r$ by marginal likelihood over the fixed grid given in
Supplement~S1.1.

Kernel and noise hyperparameters are fitted
jointly using primary and auxiliary observations,
and responses are standardized separately for
each target. Accordingly, full pooling assumes
a common activity function on the standardized
scale. Importantly, fixing $r=0$ removes direct
cross-target covariance but still allows
auxiliary observations to influence shared
hyperparameter estimates. This distinction
motivates the covariance-free control
(\mtzero{}). Further details of GP fitting
are provided in the supplement.

Compound selection uses expected improvement
(EI), computed from the posterior predictive
distribution relative to the best observed
primary-target activity, with improvement
offset $\xi=0.01$. Because EI depends on both
predictive mean and uncertainty, changes in
the fitted surrogate can alter compound
selection even without improving activity
predictions.

Each campaign begins with ten primary-target
observations. Subsequent experiments are
selected in batches of ten, using the
highest EI scores without replacement.
The posterior remains fixed within a batch
and is refitted after each batch. Acquisition
ties are resolved randomly, and the final
batch is truncated when necessary to respect
the assay budget. All methods use EI except
where an acquisition control explicitly
modifies its uncertainty input.

\subsection{Four Diagnostic Controls}
\label{sec:method-controls}

Controls C1--C4 test whether measured auxiliary responses are required
for a discovery gain and whether model fitting, acquisition behavior, or
historical compound selection can reproduce it.

\paragraph{C1. Auxiliary-response randomization.}
We perturb auxiliary responses while preserving primary-target responses
and historical observation locations. Within-target permutation retains
the empirical distribution of the responses observed in each auxiliary
target's history, but breaks their activity--compound correspondence.
Historical locations are drawn first; only their observed responses are
permuted, with one fixed permutation per target and repeat. A separate Gaussian-noise control
interpolates between measured responses ($q=1$) and randomized responses
($q=0$), with noise regenerated for each held-out target and repeat;
Supplement~S1.2 gives its construction. Unlike permutation, the Gaussian
null does not preserve the empirical marginal distribution.
Both controls are evaluated across six families under uniform histories,
with matched initial designs and historical observation locations.
This adapts response-randomization validation~\cite{rucker2007yrandomization}
to the information supplied by auxiliary targets.

\paragraph{C2. Response correspondence and joint fitting.}
At each selection round, we fit two multi-task donors at the same primary
observations and historical locations: one uses measured auxiliary responses
($M$), the other their historical-response permutation ($P$). We cross the
parameter donor with the responses used for posterior conditioning, yielding
$MM$, $MP$, $PM$, and $PP$; the first letter denotes the parameter donor.
Coupling, signal variance, noise ratio, all normalization transforms, and
numerical jitter are fixed during reconditioning. Donors are re-estimated
at every round from the observations available on that policy's trajectory.
Thus, $MM$ and $PP$ reproduce ordinary measured- and permuted-response
multi-task BO. At a shared state, $MM$ versus $MP$ changes response
correspondence without changing predictive variance, whereas $MM$ versus
$PM$ changes fitting while retaining measured conditioning responses.

We evaluate the four policies both as sequential campaigns and as next-batch
replays on shared single-task and $MM$ trajectories. The latter hold the
primary observations fixed; the former include feedback from differing
selections. Parameters learned from measured responses still carry response
information, so $MP$ is not an auxiliary-value-free control. The earlier
covariance-free check (\mtzero{}) remains a special case: fixing $r=0$ and
all appropriate single-task parameters reproduces the single-task posterior.
Supplement~S2 gives implementation details and the original JAK comparison.

\paragraph{C3. Uncertainty-adjusted acquisition.}
We retain the single-task training data and predictive means, and rescale
only the predictive standard deviation supplied to EI:
\[
\widetilde{\sigma}(m)=c\,\sigma_{\stl}(m).
\]
The scale $c$ comes from the early predictive uncertainty ratio between
fitted multi-task and single-task BO, independently of recall. This tests
whether an uncertainty change of the observed magnitude can reproduce a
gain; a single scale need not reproduce the full multi-task posterior.

\paragraph{C4. Historical compound selection.}
We compare \emph{uniform histories}, sampled without reference to
primary-target activities, with \emph{withheld histories}, which exclude
the primary target's true top-10 compounds. Withholding uses primary-target
outcomes and is a retrospective diagnostic, not a prospective sampling
strategy. Uniform histories provide the main evaluation.

Historical observations can reduce uncertainty at measured compounds and
lower their priority under EI. Avoiding history may then favor discovery
when the best compounds are withheld, but hinder it when uniform histories
include them. To test this behavior without reading auxiliary responses,
we downweight single-task EI at historical compounds and compare the
resulting selection behavior with full pooling.

\subsection{Data, Metrics, and Inference}
\label{sec:setup}

We evaluate our approach using BindingDB activity
data~\cite{bindingdb2016} from six protein families:
JAK, CYP, DPP, HDAC, MMP, and PI3K. For each family,
we construct a panel of four protein targets and
retain compounds with measured activities against
all four. This allows us to control which auxiliary
measurements are available during optimization
without confounding historical sampling with
missing assay outcomes. Each target serves in turn
as the primary optimization task, while the
remaining three provide auxiliary history.
JAK and CYP are used for detailed mechanism
analyses, and all six families are included
in the historical sampling experiments.
Requiring complete four-target measurement
makes the resulting libraries small: after
removing reporting-limit artifacts, the
campaign library holds $904$ compounds for
JAK, $590$ for PI3K, $378$ for HDAC, $335$
for CYP, $300$ for DPP, and $248$ for MMP.
These sizes bound what any method can recover
and should be read alongside every recall
figure below. Panel composition, target
identifiers, and pairwise correlations are
provided in Supplement~S1.3.

We use recorded activities without imputation,
convert IC$_{50}$ values to pIC$_{50}$ where
appropriate, and exclude reporting-limit
artifacts. Complementary experiments on KIBA~\cite{tang2014kiba},
ChEMBL~\cite{chembl2019}, and DOCKSTRING~\cite{dockstring2022}
examine assay allocation, historical-data reuse,
and initial compound selection. KIBA scores
are retained on their original scale, while
DOCKSTRING provides simulated docking scores
rather than experimental activities.
These additional analyses are reported in
the supplement.

Discovery performance is measured by top-10
recall, the proportion of the primary target's
ten most active compounds recovered within
an assay budget. Let $\mathcal H_{10}$ denote
these compounds in the constructed library
and $\mathcal S_b$ the distinct compounds
evaluated on the primary target by budget $b$.
We define
\[
\operatorname{Recall@10}(b)
=
\frac{|\mathcal S_b\cap\mathcal H_{10}|}{10}.
\]
Performance differences relative to
single-task BO are reported in percentage
points. For initial-design experiments,
we additionally use the area under the
recall curve to account for the timing
of compound discovery.

Campaigns use matched assay budgets, initial designs,
and historical samples. Repeats characterize within-panel
variation, not independent biological replicates. The two
budgets within each family share a historical draw, verified
by index hashes (Supplement~S6); cross-family inference
therefore averages budget-specific effects within each
family and uses six family-level effects in a two-sided
Wilcoxon signed-rank test. We report effect sizes, confidence
intervals, and positive-family counts; the minimum two-sided
$p$-value at $n=6$ is $0.03125$.

\subsection{Auxiliary Responses Contribute to Gains Under Uniform Histories}
\label{sec:res-null}

relative to \stl{} by $\crossMeasuredGain{}$ percentage points, compared with

\subsection{Acquisition Uncertainty Can Reproduce a Comparable JAK Gain}
\label{sec:res-exploration}

Under covariance-free joint fitting, mean predictive standard deviation
decreases from $0.643$ to $0.558$ on JAK and from $0.629$ to $0.522$ on CYP
(Supplement~S2). C3 tests whether a comparable uncertainty adjustment,
without auxiliary observations, can reproduce the discovery gain.

In a separate early-campaign diagnostic on JAK, fitted \mt{} has
approximately $0.80$ times the predictive uncertainty of \stl{} near
50 primary observations. Using this ratio to rescale only the
single-task standard deviation inside EI ($c=0.80$) produces a gain of
\valScaleMatchedJak{} $\pm$ \semScaleMatchedJak{} points, comparable to
the \valMechJakMt{}-point gain of \mt{} with randomized auxiliary
responses. With measured auxiliary responses, \mt{} still exceeds the
scaled single-task baseline by $5.31\pm0.91$ points. This residual is
consistent with useful activity information, although the comparison
does not isolate its contribution exactly. Uncertainty scaling is an
acquisition intervention, not evidence of improved calibration; existing
calibration and chemical-isolation probes are reported in Supplement~S2.

The same fixed-rescaling explanation does not reproduce the pooled CYP
effect. Across the tested scales $c\in[0.35,20]$, the largest improvement
for single-task BO is $+0.08$ points, far below the
\valMechCypPooled{}-point pooled gain under randomized responses.
Thus, changing exploration can produce a JAK gain of the observed size,
but is insufficient to account for the CYP result in these controls.

\subsection{Historical Selection Can Reverse the Pooled-Model Gain}
\label{sec:res-design}

Unlike fitted \mt{}, \pooled{} treats auxiliary measurements as
observations of the same standardized activity function as the primary
target. This can reduce uncertainty at historically assayed compounds and
change which candidates rank highest under EI. On CYP with uniform
histories, the ratio of historical-compound selection to its library
coverage is $0.288$ for \pooled{}, compared with $0.542$ for \mt{}
and $0.972$ for \stl{}. The corresponding pooled-model mean predictive
standard deviation is $0.412$ on historical compounds and $0.542$
elsewhere. The observed avoidance concerns the compounds selected, not
necessarily the average EI value over the entire historical set.

This behavior has opposite consequences under the two sampling rules.
Withheld histories exclude the primary target's true top-10 compounds,
so avoiding history can favor discovery. Uniform histories may contain
those compounds, making avoidance potentially harmful. On CYP, with
randomized auxiliary responses and 300 historical entries, switching
from withheld to uniform sampling changes the pooled recall gain from
$+22.97$ to $-23.05$ points---a reversal of approximately 46 points.
Under withholding, increasing history from 30 to 300 entries increases
the gain from $-1.25$ to $+22.97$ points. A separate check at
150 entries under uniform sampling finds similar effects for permuted
and randomized auxiliary responses ($-19.69$ and $-18.52$ points,
respectively), supporting the interpretation that the original
activity--compound correspondence is not required.

\paragraph{Auxiliary-value-free avoidance control.}
We calibrate the single-task EI penalty $W$ on historically

\subsection{The Value of Existing History Differs from New Auxiliary Assays}
\label{sec:res-sharing}
Existing historical measurements do not consume new
primary-target assays. By contrast, allocating new assays
to related targets reduces primary-target predictive rank
correlation in the tested settings. Reusing history is most
useful when primary data are scarce and offers little benefit
on low-overlap ChEMBL; history can also guide initial
compound selection without entering the GP. These supplementary
analyses use predictive rank correlation or recall-curve AUC,
not the main Recall@10 metric (Supplement~S3--S4).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
\label{sec:limits}

The crossed controls show that the value of auxiliary response correspondence
depends on the fit used to interpret it. Measured-response fitting helps
when conditioning retains measured responses, but hurts after permutation
in all six families. Thus, response-informed fitting is itself a route of
useful auxiliary information, and its benefit depends on the conditioning
responses. The shared-state replays distinguish response-dependent selection
from changes in predictive variance; sequential campaigns assess the
resulting discovery performance as observations accumulate.

For screening teams evaluating historical assay reuse, these comparisons
can test whether an apparent gain depends on activity information or can
be reproduced through fitting, acquisition, or compound selection. Within
the shared-coupling model class studied here, the results favor fitted
multi-task BO over assuming full pooling. They support an evaluation
protocol; prospective assay savings remain to be established. Reusing
existing history also differs from purchasing new auxiliary assays.

\paragraph{A reporting protocol.}
We recommend pairing discovery comparisons with the four diagnostic
controls. Compare measured and within-target permuted auxiliary responses
at matched historical observation locations, preserving the observed
response multisets, to assess the contribution of activity correspondence.
Then cross response conditions with locked response-informed fits to compare
conditioning and fitting at shared states and in sequential campaigns. When gains persist after randomization,
use covariance-free joint fitting and uncertainty-adjusted single-task BO
to test alternative modeling explanations. Compare historical sampling
designs and an auxiliary-response-free avoidance policy to assess sensitivity
to compound selection. Positive measured-versus-permuted contrasts support
informative auxiliary responses; successful alternative controls establish
that a gain need not arise solely from cross-target transfer. No single
control identifies a unique causal mechanism, and the comparisons do not
provide a causal decomposition of the observed effects.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Our experiments replay recorded activities in constructed libraries rather
than test compounds prospectively. Requiring measurements against all four
targets may favor well-studied chemical series and limits generalization to
sparse screening libraries. Recall@10 measures recovery of top-ranked
compounds within each library, not an absolute activity or selectivity
threshold. The study covers six protein families; repeated random seeds
measure within-panel variation, not independent biological replication.

The fixed ECFP4 representation and single shared coupling parameter $r$
restrict the model class. We do not test learned molecular representations
or models with a separate relationship for each target pair. Neither uniform
nor withheld histories fully represents the original collection of assay
records. The controls reproduce gains of the observed magnitude without
quantifying each mechanism's contribution to an individual gain. Sequential
crossed-policy contrasts also include differences in acquired primary
observations; matched-history replays isolate one-step decisions instead.

\paragraph{Future directions.}
Prospective screening and more realistic historical sampling would test
whether these findings extend beyond retrospective libraries. More flexible
task covariance models, including priors from protein language models or
structural similarity, could inform target relationships when activity data
are scarce. The same controls can assess whether such extensions improve
discovery through informative transfer or through changes in fitting and
compound selection.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Crossing auxiliary responses with locked fits reveals an interaction between
response correspondence and response-informed parameter estimation in
molecular BO. Across six retrospective libraries, measured-response fitting
helps with measured conditioning responses but hurts after permutation,
while full pooling remains sensitive to historical sampling. Evaluations
of historical assay reuse should pair discovery performance with these
crossed controls and sampling checks to interpret where gains arise.
