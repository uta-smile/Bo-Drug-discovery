\begin{abstract}
Historical assays from related protein targets are widely used to guide
molecular Bayesian optimization (BO), but improved hit recovery does not
necessarily indicate transferable activity information. Auxiliary responses can inform both model fitting and posterior predictions,
while historical observation locations can affect compound selection
independently of response correspondence. We investigate these effects through
retrospective Gaussian-process BO experiments on BindingDB libraries spanning
six protein families. Under matched assay budgets and historical compound
locations, we compare single-task, fitted multi-task, and fully pooled BO. To
distinguish the contributions of auxiliary responses during model fitting and
posterior conditioning, we systematically cross measured and permuted
historical responses in matched-history replays and sequential campaigns.

With uniformly sampled histories, fitted multi-task BO improves Recall@10
across all six families. However, the crossed experiments reveal a critical
interaction: fitting with measured auxiliary responses improves recall when
conditioning also uses measured responses, but reduces recall when
conditioning uses permuted responses. Matched-history replays further show
changes in compound selection despite unchanged predictive variance.
Additional controls reproduce improvements in selected settings, while full
pooling remains sensitive to historical sampling design.

Improved hit recovery alone is therefore insufficient evidence of
cross-target information transfer. The conditions matter more than the size
of the gain: under uniform histories, fitted multi-task BO improves recall in
all six families, whereas full pooling improves it in four and its
family-level interval includes zero. Evaluations of auxiliary assay reuse
should separate the effects of historical data on model fitting, posterior
conditioning, and sequential selection.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Identifying active compounds for a protein target requires screening molecular
libraries under limited experimental budgets. Bayesian optimization (BO)
uses a surrogate model and an acquisition function to select compounds for
evaluation~\cite{jones1998ego,shahriari2016taking}. With few primary-target
observations, both predictions and selection decisions depend strongly on
model assumptions. Historical assays from related targets offer additional
information without consuming new primary-target assays, and multi-task BO
uses these measurements to guide the search~\cite{zhao2020multitask,swersky2013multitask}.

A gain in compound discovery does not, by itself, establish that auxiliary
activity information contributes to that gain. Auxiliary responses enter
both parameter estimation and posterior conditioning. Joint fitting can
change predictive means and uncertainty, while historical observation
locations can alter compound selection independently of the measured
responses. Evaluations based only on recall conflate these effects.

Our central question is how the correspondence between auxiliary activities
and compounds affects discovery through model fitting, posterior conditioning,
and their interaction. We address it in retrospective Gaussian-process BO
on six BindingDB protein families. Within the same model, we cross measured
and permuted auxiliary responses used for fitting with those used for
conditioning, at matched historical observation locations
(Fig.~\ref{fig:design}). Sequential campaigns measure discovery as
observations accumulate; matched-history replays compare immediate selection
at shared primary-target observations. Complementary controls examine
acquisition uncertainty and historical compound selection.

The crossed evaluation reveals that measured-response fitting helps discovery
with measured conditioning responses but hurts with permuted responses.
Shared-state replays show that response correspondence changes candidate
selection even when predictive variance is fixed. Full pooling is additionally
sensitive to how historical compounds are sampled. These findings motivate
evaluating the information and decisions behind a gain, alongside its size.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item We develop a crossed evaluation of auxiliary-response correspondence
in GP fitting and posterior conditioning, with locked parameters and
normalization transforms, matched-history replay, and sequential campaigns.
\item Across six retrospective protein-family libraries under uniform
histories, we identify a fitting--conditioning interaction and show that
response-dependent means change selection at fixed predictive variance
in shared-state replays.
\item We test complementary explanations through acquisition and sampling
controls, including an outcome-informed stress test in which historical
compound avoidance reproduces much of full pooling's design sensitivity.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and Experimental Framework}
\label{sec:framework}

\subsection{Prior Work and the Diagnostic Question}
\label{sec:related}

Molecular BO and active learning prioritize compound evaluations under
limited budgets~\cite{graff2021accelerating,gao2022pmo}. Multifidelity BO
combines computational and experimental measurements of different
costs~\cite{mcdonald2025multifidelity}; here, auxiliary assays are existing
measurements from related protein targets.

Multi-task GPs couple tasks through a shared input kernel and task
covariance~\cite{bonilla2007multitask,swersky2013multitask}. Biological BO
already distinguishes shared kernel hyperparameters from correlated function
values~\cite{sedgwick2025transfer}, and HyperBO learns GP priors from earlier
tasks~\cite{wang2024hyperbo}. Hvarfner et al.~\cite{hvarfner2026pitfalls}
analyze how standardization and observation overlap affect task-correlation
inference. Our diagnostic crosses the response condition used for fitting
and conditioning within the same molecular model, then evaluates both
shared-state acquisition choices and sequential discovery. The contribution
is this controlled comparison and its empirical interaction, rather than
the distinction between parameter and function-value transfer itself.

Response randomization is established in molecular-model
validation~\cite{rucker2007yrandomization}. Training-set composition and
cross-target relationships also affect biological optimization and benchmark
validity~\cite{wittmann2021informed,leakage2026}. We preserve the responses
actually observed in each auxiliary history when permuting their compound
correspondence, and examine historical observation locations separately.

\subsection{Experimental Setting and Assay Budget}
\label{sec:matrix}

We consider Bayesian optimization over a fixed compound
library $\mathcal M$, with one primary protein target
$t_0$ and related targets $t_1,\ldots,t_{T-1}$ providing
historical activity measurements. Let $y(m,t_j)$ denote
the activity of compound $m$ against target $t_j$,
with larger values indicating greater activity.
Available measurements are represented by a
compound--target observation mask
$M\in\{0,1\}^{N\times T}$; missing entries are
not imputed.

In each retrospective campaign, primary-target
activities are revealed only when their compounds
are selected for evaluation. Discovery performance
is measured against the ten most active compounds
in the constructed library, rather than against
an external or exhaustive chemical space.

We distinguish two uses of auxiliary measurements.
In the main transfer experiments, historical assays
have already been collected and do not consume the
new screening budget. Each newly evaluated
compound--target pair counts as one assay, and
the entire new budget is allocated to the primary
target. Assay allocation is a separate decision, examined in Supplement~S3.



\subsection{Models and Acquisition}
\label{sec:models}

Each observed compound--target entry supplies a training example
$((\mathbf{x}_m,t_j),y(m,t_j))$. The same compound may appear at several
targets with different responses. We use fixed 2048-bit ECFP4
fingerprints~\cite{rogers2010ecfp,rdkit} and a Tanimoto kernel, without
learned molecular features or protein sequence and structure features.
Cross-target dependence enters only through the task covariance matrix.

We compare three GP surrogates. Single-task
BO (\stl{}) uses only primary-target
measurements. Fitted multi-task BO (\mt{})
uses both primary and auxiliary measurements,
with cross-target dependence learned from the
data. Fully pooled BO (\pooled{}) uses the
same measurements but assumes complete
dependence across targets.

For the multi-task model, the covariance
between compound--target pairs is
\[
k\big((m,a),(m',b)\big)
=
k_{\mathrm{Tanimoto}}(m,m')B_{ab},
\qquad
B=(1-r)I+r\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}.
\]
The parameter $r$ controls the common
dependence between different targets.
At $r=0$, their direct covariance vanishes;
at $r=1$, they are modeled as fully coupled.
Thus, \mt{} estimates one shared coupling
parameter rather than a separate relationship
for each pair of proteins.

We select $r$ by marginal likelihood over the fixed grid given in
Supplement~S1.1.

Kernel and noise hyperparameters are fitted
jointly using primary and auxiliary observations,
and responses are standardized separately for
each target. Accordingly, full pooling assumes
a common activity function on the standardized
scale. Importantly, fixing $r=0$ removes direct
cross-target covariance but still allows
auxiliary observations to influence shared
hyperparameter estimates. This distinction
motivates the covariance-free control
(\mtzero{}). Further details of GP fitting
are provided in the supplement.

Compound selection uses expected improvement
(EI), computed from the posterior predictive
distribution relative to the best observed
primary-target activity, with improvement
offset $\xi=0.01$. Because EI depends on both
predictive mean and uncertainty, changes in
the fitted surrogate can alter compound
selection even without improving activity
predictions.

Each campaign begins with ten primary-target
observations. Subsequent experiments are
selected in batches of ten, using the highest
EI scores without replacement; the posterior
remains fixed within a batch and is refitted
after each batch. Acquisition ties are
resolved randomly. The final batch is
truncated when necessary to respect the assay
budget, and all methods use EI except where an
acquisition control explicitly modifies its
uncertainty input.

\subsection{Data, Metrics, and Inference}
\label{sec:setup}

We evaluate our approach using BindingDB activity
data~\cite{bindingdb2016} from six protein families:
JAK, CYP, DPP, HDAC, MMP, and PI3K. For each family,
we construct a panel of four protein targets and
retain compounds with measured activities against
all four. Complete panels let us control which
auxiliary measurements are available during
optimization, so historical sampling is not
confounded with missing assay outcomes. Each target
serves in turn as the primary optimization task,
while the remaining three provide auxiliary history.
All six families enter the crossed evaluation. JAK
and CYP also support detailed acquisition and
historical-selection diagnostics.
Requiring complete four-target measurement
makes the resulting libraries small: after
removing reporting-limit artifacts, the
campaign library holds $904$ compounds for
JAK, $590$ for PI3K, $378$ for HDAC, $335$
for CYP, $300$ for DPP, and $248$ for MMP.
These sizes bound what any method can recover
and should be read alongside every recall
figure below. Panel composition, target
identifiers, and pairwise correlations are
provided in Supplement~S1.3.

We use recorded activities without imputation,
convert IC$_{50}$ values to pIC$_{50}$ where
appropriate, and exclude reporting-limit
artifacts. Panel construction and preprocessing are detailed in Supplement~S1;
secondary studies of assay allocation and initial selection are in
Supplement~S3--S4.

Discovery performance is measured by top-10
recall, the proportion of the primary target's
ten most active compounds recovered within
an assay budget. Let $\mathcal H_{10}$ denote
these compounds in the constructed library
and $\mathcal S_b$ the distinct compounds
evaluated on the primary target by budget $b$.
We define
\[
\operatorname{Recall@10}(b)
=
\frac{|\mathcal S_b\cap\mathcal H_{10}|}{10}.
\]
Performance differences relative to
single-task BO are reported in percentage
points. For initial-design experiments,
we additionally use the area under the
recall curve to account for the timing
of compound discovery.

The crossed evaluation uses 32 repeats for each of four primary targets
at two assay budgets per family, with 50 historical observations per
auxiliary target. Campaigns use matched assay budgets, initial designs,
and historical samples. Repeats characterize within-panel
variation, not independent biological replicates. The two
budgets within each family share a historical draw, verified
by index hashes (Supplement~S6); cross-family inference
therefore first averages targets and repeats within each budget, then
averages the two budget-specific effects within each family and uses six family-level effects in a two-sided
Wilcoxon signed-rank test. We report effect sizes, confidence
intervals, and positive-family counts; the minimum two-sided
$p$-value at $n=6$ is $0.03125$.

\section{Diagnostic Design}
\label{sec:method}
\label{sec:method-controls}

An auxiliary history contains observed compound--target locations
$\mathcal L=\{(m,t_j):M_{mj}=1,\ j\geq1\}$, responses $y_{\mathrm{aux}}$,
and a sampling rule $\pi$. We ask whether response correspondence improves
discovery (C1), how it interacts with fitting (C2), and whether acquisition
or historical selection can reproduce gains (C3--C4). C2 is the central
experiment; the other controls establish its context and test complementary
explanations. These interventions compare model and policy behavior, not
unique biological mechanisms or additive causal shares of a total gain.

\subsection{Response Correspondence and Fitting}
\paragraph{C1. Auxiliary-response randomization.}
We perturb auxiliary responses while preserving primary-target responses
and historical observation locations. Within-target permutation retains
the empirical distribution of the responses observed in each auxiliary
target's history, but breaks their activity--compound correspondence.
Historical locations are drawn first; only their observed responses are
permuted, with one fixed permutation per target and repeat. A separate Gaussian-noise control
interpolates between measured responses ($q=1$) and randomized responses
($q=0$), with noise regenerated for each held-out target and repeat;
Supplement~S1.2 gives its construction. Unlike permutation, the Gaussian
null does not preserve the empirical marginal distribution.
Both controls are evaluated across six families under uniform histories,
with matched initial designs and historical observation locations.
This adapts response-randomization validation~\cite{rucker2007yrandomization}
to the information supplied by auxiliary targets.

\paragraph{C2. Response correspondence and joint fitting.}
At each selection round, we fit two multi-task donors at the same primary
observations and historical locations: one uses measured auxiliary responses
($M$), the other their historical-response permutation ($P$). We cross the
parameter donor with the responses used for posterior conditioning, yielding
$MM$, $MP$, $PM$, and $PP$; the first letter denotes the parameter donor.
Coupling, signal variance, noise ratio, all normalization transforms, and
numerical jitter are fixed during reconditioning. Donors are re-estimated
at every round from the observations available on that policy's trajectory.
Thus, $MM$ and $PP$ reproduce ordinary measured- and permuted-response
multi-task BO. At a shared state with fixed historical locations, parameters,
and normalization transforms, changing conditioning responses leaves GP
predictive variance unchanged. This standard posterior property makes
$MM$ versus $MP$ a comparison of response-dependent means at fixed variance.
By contrast, $MM$ versus $PM$ changes fitting with measured conditioning
responses retained. Variance equality does not apply across independently
evolving sequential trajectories.

We evaluate the four policies both as sequential campaigns and as next-batch
replays on shared single-task and $MM$ trajectories. The latter hold the
primary observations fixed; the former include feedback from differing
selections. Parameters learned from measured responses still carry response
information, so $MP$ is not an auxiliary-value-free control. The earlier
covariance-free check (\mtzero{}) remains a special case: fixing $r=0$ and
all appropriate single-task parameters reproduces the single-task posterior.
Supplement~S2 gives implementation details and the original JAK comparison.

\subsection{Acquisition and Historical Sampling}

\paragraph{C3. Uncertainty-adjusted acquisition.}
We retain the single-task training data and predictive means, and rescale
only the predictive standard deviation supplied to EI:
\[
\widetilde{\sigma}(m)=c\,\sigma_{\stl}(m).
\]
The scale $c$ comes from the early predictive uncertainty ratio between
fitted multi-task and single-task BO, independently of recall. This tests
whether an uncertainty change of the observed magnitude can reproduce a
gain; a single scale need not reproduce the full multi-task posterior.

\paragraph{C4. Historical compound selection.}
We compare \emph{uniform histories}, sampled without reference to
primary-target activities, with \emph{withheld histories}, which exclude
the primary target's true top-10 compounds. Withholding uses primary-target
outcomes and is a retrospective diagnostic, not a prospective sampling
strategy. Uniform histories provide the main evaluation.

Historical observations can reduce uncertainty at measured compounds and
lower their priority under EI. Avoiding history may then favor discovery
when the best compounds are withheld, but hinder it when uniform histories
include them. To test this behavior without reading auxiliary responses,
we downweight single-task EI at historical compounds and compare the
resulting selection behavior with full pooling.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Auxiliary Responses Contribute to Gains Under Uniform Histories}
\label{sec:res-null}

Under uniform histories, fitted multi-task BO ($MM$) improves recall
relative to \stl{} by $\crossMeasuredGain{}$ percentage points, compared with
$\crossPermutedGain{}$ after permutation of the observed historical responses
($PP$). The paired measured-minus-permuted contrast is $\crossPrimary{}$
points (95\% CI $\crossPrimaryCi{}$), positive in
$\crossPrimaryPositive{}/6$ families ($p=\crossPrimaryP{}$).
Historical locations and the exact response multiset for each auxiliary
target are unchanged, so the comparison tests response correspondence
without changing the observed response distributions. Each condition uses
\crossCampaigns{} campaigns per family and budget; inference averages the
two budgets within each family. The new runs reproduce the original
single-task and measured-response recall vectors exactly. Supplement~S2
reports per-budget values and legacy permutation diagnostics.

\subsection{The Benefit of Response-Informed Fitting Depends on Posterior Conditioning}
\label{sec:res-mech}

Table~\ref{tab:crossed} compares the crossed policies under uniform histories.
With the measured-response fit retained, measured rather than permuted
conditioning responses improve recall by
$\crossResponseMeasuredFit{}$ points ($MM-MP$; 95\% CI
$\crossResponseMeasuredFitCi{}$). With the permuted-response fit retained,
the corresponding contrast is $\crossResponsePermutedFit{}$ points
($PM-PP$; $\crossResponsePermutedFitCi{}$). Conversely, changing the fit
while retaining measured responses gives $\crossFitMeasuredResponse{}$
points ($MM-PM$; $\crossFitMeasuredResponseCi{}$), and with permuted
responses gives $\crossFitPermutedResponse{}$ points
($MP-PP$; $\crossFitPermutedResponseCi{}$). Thus, the measured-response
fit improves discovery when conditioning retains measured responses, but
hurts when conditioning uses permuted responses.

The predefined descriptive contrasts yield a factorial interaction of

Matched-history replay provides a complementary check:
on shared single-task trajectories, $MM$ and $MP$ recover

the corresponding means are $\crossMtAnchorMMBatchHits{}$ and

not a decomposition of final sequential recall. The original covariance-free

\subsection{Uncertainty Rescaling Alone Reproduces the JAK Gain but Not the CYP Effect}
\label{sec:res-exploration}

Under covariance-free joint fitting, mean predictive standard deviation
decreases from $0.643$ to $0.558$ on JAK and from $0.629$ to $0.522$ on CYP
(Supplement~S2). C3 tests whether a comparable uncertainty adjustment,
without auxiliary observations, can reproduce the discovery gain.

In a separate early-campaign diagnostic on JAK, fitted \mt{} has
approximately $0.80$ times the predictive uncertainty of \stl{} near
50 primary observations. Using this ratio to rescale only the
single-task standard deviation inside EI ($c=0.80$) produces a gain of
\valScaleMatchedJak{} $\pm$ \semScaleMatchedJak{} points, comparable to
the \valMechJakMt{}-point gain of \mt{} with randomized auxiliary
responses. With measured auxiliary responses, \mt{} still exceeds the
scaled single-task baseline by $5.31\pm0.91$ points. This residual is
consistent with useful activity information, although the comparison
does not isolate its contribution exactly. Uncertainty scaling is an
acquisition intervention, not evidence of improved calibration; existing
calibration and chemical-isolation probes are reported in Supplement~S2.

The same fixed-rescaling explanation does not reproduce the pooled CYP
effect. Across the tested scales $c\in[0.35,20]$, the largest improvement
for single-task BO is $+0.08$ points, far below the
\valMechCypPooled{}-point pooled gain under randomized responses.
Thus, changing exploration can produce a JAK gain of the observed size,
but is insufficient to account for the CYP result in these controls.
A baseline matched on model class but not on exploration therefore
overstates what auxiliary responses contribute.

\subsection{Historical Sampling Design Reverses the Benefit of Full Pooling}
\label{sec:res-design}

\paragraph{Outcome-informed withholding as a stress test.}
The withheld-history experiments test sensitivity to historical sampling. Even after auxiliary

Such a reduction can change which candidates rank highest under EI. On CYP with uniform

necessarily the average EI value over the entire historical set.

This behavior has opposite consequences under the two sampling rules.
Withheld histories exclude the primary target's true top-10 compounds,
so avoiding history can favor discovery. Uniform histories may contain
those compounds, making avoidance potentially harmful. On CYP, with

activity--compound correspondence is not required.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
\label{sec:limits}

\paragraph{Interpreting auxiliary information.}
The fitting--conditioning interaction shows why auxiliary responses should
be evaluated through both routes. Parameters learned from measured responses
carry information even when later conditioning uses permuted responses;
the resulting fit need not remain useful under that change. Matched-history
replay tests the immediate decisions induced by response-dependent means,
whereas sequential campaigns include feedback from differing observations.
Within the shared-coupling model class, the results favor fitted multi-task
BO over assuming full pooling, whose benefit is more sensitive to history
design. They do not establish prospective assay savings.

\paragraph{An evaluation protocol.}
For historical assay reuse, first compare measured and within-target permuted
responses at matched locations, preserving the observed response multisets.
Then cross fitting and conditioning to test their interaction, using shared
states for immediate acquisition comparisons and separate campaigns for
sequential performance. If gains persist after randomization, covariance-free
joint fitting and uncertainty-adjusted acquisition test complementary
explanations. Finally, compare sampling designs and a response-free avoidance
policy to assess dependence on historical compound selection. A successful
control shows that a gain can be reproduced by that intervention; the
controls do not assign unique mechanisms or causal percentages to the total
gain. Uncertainty scaling changes acquisition and does not establish improved
calibration~\cite{foldager2023uncertainty}.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Our experiments are retrospective. We replay recorded activities in
constructed libraries rather than testing compounds prospectively.
Requiring measurements against all four
targets may favor well-studied chemical series and limits generalization to
sparse screening libraries. Recall@10 measures recovery of top-ranked
compounds within each library, not an absolute activity or selectivity
threshold. The study covers six protein families. Repeated random seeds
measure within-panel variation, not independent biological replication.

The fixed ECFP4 representation and single shared coupling parameter $r$
restrict the model class. We do not test learned molecular representations
or models with a separate relationship for each target pair. Neither uniform
nor withheld histories fully represents the original collection of assay
records. Withholding uses primary-target outcomes and is a retrospective
stress test. Sequential crossed-policy contrasts include differences in
acquired primary observations; matched-history replays isolate one-step
decisions instead. Reusing existing history also differs from purchasing new
auxiliary assays, as examined in Supplement~S3.

\paragraph{Future directions.}
Prospective screening and more realistic historical sampling would test
whether the interaction extends beyond retrospective libraries. More
flexible task covariance models, including priors from protein sequence or
structural similarity, could inform relationships when activity data are
scarce. The crossed evaluation can test how such extensions use response
information in fitting and selection.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Auxiliary data helps molecular BO, but only under conditions that recall
alone does not reveal. Measured auxiliary responses carry information:
under uniform histories, fitted multi-task BO improves recall in every
family, and permuting the response--compound correspondence removes part
of that gain. The benefit is conditional on the posterior, since fitting
on measured responses helps when conditioning retains them and hurts when
it does not, and matched-history replay changes the proposed batch at
fixed predictive variance. Two further routes produce gains without any
transferred activity: rescaling single-task acquisition uncertainty
reproduces a JAK gain of comparable size, and avoiding historically
assayed compounds reproduces much of the design sensitivity of full
pooling while reading no auxiliary values. A reported recall gain thus
measures the fit, the conditioning, the acquisition and the sampling
design together. For historical assay reuse, fitted coupling is the more
dependable choice, its benefit should be read against an
exploration-matched baseline, and the provenance of the history belongs
in the report alongside the gain.
